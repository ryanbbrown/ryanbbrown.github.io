An FDE’s Field Guide to EVV Data

Federal law requires electronic visit verification (EVV) for Medicaid home care: each visit must prove six facts. The law does not define a file format or field names. Each aggregator and state publishes its own layout, 30 to 141 fields wide, and the differences between those layouts matter operationally.

The layouts all record the same six facts under different field names, which is why this guide calls them dialects. It is organized around the six federal facts, showing how four published dialects express each one. Each numbered section covers one decision an integrations engineer has to make, with the field evidence under it.

Why the differences, not the fields, are the subject

This guide assumes the differences between dialects matter operationally, and are not trivia a field dictionary would cover. The strongest evidence comes from people working on this data. Zingage’s engineering team, which runs coordination agents on exactly this data, describes rebuilding its data layer after failures like voice AI calling caregivers about visits that were already closed. Their diagnosis: none of these were application bugs, only “contradictions born from stale or conflicting data.” Another of their posts says it directly: “A race condition isn’t an edge case - it’s a missed visit.”

State regulators treat the differences the same way. Pennsylvania requires 85% of visits to verify automatically, with no manual edits, so it measures the gap between automatic and edited verification. Texas regulates edits with a 95-day window and mandatory reason codes. And an unverified visit cannot be billed to Medicaid, so a mistake in reading verification data costs the agency the payment.

The dialects themselves make the same point: every one carries three separate timestamp pairs, and the dialects that model missed visits at all give them their own code tables, separate from the edit codes. Vendors would not carry all of that if the distinctions did not matter.

Who’s who in a home-care visit

  • Home care. Help with the tasks of daily living, bathing, dressing, meals, delivered in the client’s own home.
  • The client. The person receiving care. The specs below also call them the member or the individual.
  • The caregiver. The person who shows up and does the visit. Employed by an agency.
  • The agency. A business that employs caregivers, schedules them into clients’ homes, and bills for the hours delivered.
  • Medicaid. Government health insurance, run by each state under federal rules; in this guide the bill always goes to Medicaid. The money moves in one direction: Medicaid pays the agency for each visit, and the agency pays the caregiver.
  • The EMR. The agency’s scheduling system (EMR is short for electronic medical record). Knows who should be in which home at what hour.
  • The EVV system. Records what actually happened: the caregiver’s clock-in and clock-out, with the evidence attached. Every agency runs one of two kinds: the capture system its state provides, or its own, either built into the EMR or a separate product.
  • The aggregator. Run by the state. Collects visit records from every agency and checks claims against them before Medicaid pays.
  • The vendors. The software companies in this business: Sandata, HHAeXchange, and Netsmart all appear below. A vendor is a company, not a role in the pipeline: the same company can sell software to agencies in one state and run the state’s aggregator in another. Three of the four dialects below are the intake layouts of vendors hired as state aggregators; the fourth, Texas, is published by the state itself.

This page is about the last hand-off: getting the visit into the state’s aggregator. An agency on the state-provided system never sees this hand-off; the state’s system feeds the aggregator internally. An agency running its own EVV system, the “alternate EVV” case these specs are written for, must submit every visit in the aggregator’s published layout. Four aggregators, four layouts: those are the dialects below.

The four dialects

How a visit turns into one of these records: during the visit, the caregiver clocks in and out through whatever their agency runs, a mobile app that records GPS, a phone call from the client’s home number, or a fixed device in the home. Those clock events land in the agency’s EVV system, which then submits the finished visit record to the state’s aggregator. Each specification below is an aggregator’s published intake layout: the format a third-party (“alternate EVV”) system must write when it submits visits. Typing a visit in after the fact is also possible, but it is the weakest form of verification and the one states cap. 02 verification

Which layout an agency writes is the state’s decision, not the agency’s. Each state Medicaid program designates an aggregator: Sandata in roughly 20 states, HHAeXchange for New York and others, Netsmart for Florida and Louisiana, Texas running its own through TMHP. So an agency operating in two states writes two dialects, and one state can route different programs or payers to different aggregators: Pennsylvania, for example, sends some programs through HHAeXchange and others through Netsmart. One vendor can also hold different roles in different states: in Texas, HHAeXchange is the state-provided system caregivers clock in through, while TMHP runs the aggregator every visit record must reach.

Every claim in this guide is drawn from a published specification you can download today. Sources and versions are at the bottom. The four tags below mark every field name that follows.

Why these four dialects

Selection rule: aggregator dialects with published, field-level specifications, chosen so that together they span the kinds of layout an integrations engineer meets in practice: a REST API with JSON records (Sandata, HHAeXchange), a positional state flat file (Texas), and a batch file drop over SFTP (Netsmart). Sandata altEVV is the generic contract used by roughly 20 states. HHAeXchange runs the largest aggregation network. Texas publishes the richest self-contained state layout in the country.

HHAeXchange also owns Sandata (it bought the company), and the two still count as separate dialects because each state wrote a specific format into its own rules. A Sandata state tells providers: submit visits to our aggregator in the altEVV layout, using our published list of code values. A state that designated HHAeXchange instead tells providers to submit through the HHAeXchange API. Those state rules did not change when one vendor bought the other, so both formats remain in force. That is the guide’s point in miniature: the schema you write is set by the state program you bill, not by who owns the software.

Agency-side EMRs are deliberately not one of the four. They record the same visit with almost none of these fields, which is the point of the aside near the bottom of this page.

The six federal concepts

Section 12006 of the 21st Century Cures Act requires an EVV system to verify six things: the type of service, the individual receiving it, the date, the location, the individual providing it, and the time it begins and ends. CMS calls the list a floor and left the layout to states and vendors. Open a concept to see its four spellings and what the differences mean.

Why the guide is organized by the six federal concepts

The six elements are the only constant across every dialect, because they are the statutory floor each layout must implement. Organizing by them lets a reader keep one mental model and learn four spellings of it, instead of learning four schemas. The rejected alternative was organizing by vendor.

The cost is uneven fit. Verification method maps cleanly onto all four dialects. Date of service is a real field only in Texas (VISIT_VISITDATE); the other three give you only timestamps, so the service day has to be computed, and an overnight visit can land on either side of midnight (concept 3). The awkward rows stay, because the awkwardness is itself information about the dialect.

01The three timestamp families

A visit record answers “when did this happen” three ways, and each answer has a different job. Detecting a missed visit means comparing scheduled with actual. Billing uses adjusted. And a difference between actual and adjusted means a person edited the record, which states cap.

Three of the four dialects store these in UTC with a separate timezone field; the Texas layout uses local Central Time instead. The three pairs are not copies of one fact. They record three facts: the schedule, the clock events, and the times the payer bills against. To be explicit about the money: a claim bills the payer for units of time, and those units are computed from the adjusted start and end. The dollars paid are a direct function of the span between those two timestamps, not the raw clock events. That is why a gap between actual and adjusted is worth money and attention, and why states watch it.

HHAeXchange caveat: the API carries both the raw Evv.clockIn/clockOut.callDateTime values and a top-level visitStartDateTime / visitEndDateTime. The spec’s field descriptions support reading the top-level two as the confirmed, billable times: if a top-level time does not match the EVV clock value, the visit “will be considered manually edited,” and a top-level time with no EVV call behind it is “considered manually confirmed.” Checked against the Illinois spec PDF on July 5, 2026.

The compliance stakes are explicit. Pennsylvania requires 85% of visits to verify automatically, with no manual edits. Texas permits edits for 95 calendar days after the visit, each with a mandatory reason code. Both states track how often actual and adjusted differ.

Why this matters at 3 AMThe clock-in feed is the missed-visit detector. If the scheduled start passes with no clock-in, a grace window runs out and a coordinator starts calling for a replacement. For a one-hour morning medication visit, the whole visit can be missed before anyone reacts. A detector built on the wrong timestamp pair fires late or not at all.

Where the three-family mapping comes from

The scheduled/actual/adjusted reading comes from the specs’ own naming and the states’ rules. Netsmart names its third pair Invoice* and Texas names it BILL_TIME, so the billing link is written into the field names. Pennsylvania and Texas regulate the gap between actual and adjusted directly, which is what makes that gap a compliance signal.

For the detection half: Zingage’s coordinator-timeline post opens on a 3 AM no-show discovered exactly this way, and describes the missing clock-in as the emergency itself: “a missing clock-in is a missing caregiver is a patient who may or may not have gotten their meds.”

The one interpretive leap here, HHAeXchange’s top-level times, was checked against the Illinois spec’s field descriptions on July 5, 2026, and held up: the spec treats a mismatch between those times and the EVV clock values as a manual edit, and a top-level time with no EVV call behind it as manually confirmed.

02Verification method: the most divergent enum

The four dialects use four incompatible vocabularies for the same question: how do we know the caregiver was really there? Do not just map the strings to each other. Rank the values by strength of evidence, because the weakest kind, manual entry, is the one regulators cap.

SANDCallTypeMobileTelephonyFVVManualOther
TXEVV_INPUTMETHOD_IN / _OUTMobile MethodLandlineAlternative DeviceGUI
NSStartVerificationType / EndVerificationTypeGPSIVRNON

Each row lists one dialect’s values, strongest evidence first. The number on each value is its rung on the trust ladder below. Yellow marks the bottom rung, the kind states cap. Sandata’s Other gets no number because it does not commit to a method; the fields that come with it decide what it is.

The trust ladder

  1. GPS inside the geofence (Mobile, Mobile Method, GPS). Device, location, and time agree. Companions: MobileLogin, a lat/long pair.
  2. Telephony from the client’s registered landline (Telephony, Landline, IVR). The originating number is the evidence. Companions: TelephonyPIN, OriginatingPhoneNumber.
  3. Fixed device in the home (FVV, FOB, Alternative Device). A code-generating token proves presence more loosely. Companion: a device ID such as EVV_ALTERNATIVEDEVICEID.
  4. Manual entry, GUI entry, or no verification (Manual, GUI, NON). A human typed it, or nothing verified that end of the visit. This is the rung states cap and the one that demands reason codes.

The ordering is this guide’s judgment, not a spec’s; no spec ranks its own values. Each rung is placed by what the method proves on its own. The one debatable call is telephony above fixed device; a reasonable integrator could swap those two. The bottom rung is the one that matters either way, because manual share is the quantity states cap.

When you translate these four vocabularies into one common field, keep the proof, not just the answer. Each value is backed by other fields on the record: a Telephony clock-in carries the phone number it came from, a Mobile or GPS one carries coordinates. Store the value without those fields and you can still say the visit was verified, but you can no longer show why. And a value that arrives without its backing fields is itself a red flag worth surfacing.

Two differences deserve attention. Netsmart verifies each end of the visit independently, so a visit can be GPS-verified in and unverified out. And the HHAeXchange API enum has no Manual value, but that does not mean manual entry cannot happen there. Per the spec, a visit time with no EVV call behind it is “considered manually confirmed,” and a changed one is “considered manually edited,” with the edit passed as reason codes (05). The manual rung exists in every dialect; HHAeXchange just keeps it outside this enum.

To be precise about who requires what: the federal Cures Act mandates EVV but sets no numeric limit on manual entry. The caps are state policy. Pennsylvania requires 85% of visits to verify automatically with no manual edits, and Texas polices edits through mandatory reason codes and a 95-day correction window (05).

Why this matters at 3 AMManual share is the leading indicator. A rising Manual or GUI percentage means an agency is drifting toward its state’s edit cap weeks before any report says so, and it points at the caregivers whose clock-ins keep failing.

03Where authorization lives

A delivered visit outside an active authorization is care the agency gave away for free. And no two dialects let you check billability the same way, because they do not agree on where the authorization lives in the data, or whether it exists as its own record at all.

An authorization is the payer’s advance approval of care for one client, and it is three constraints at once: a service type, a unit budget, and a date window (for example: personal care, up to 6 hours a week, for 12 weeks). All three must be true at the time of the visit. And authorized hours are use-it-or-lose-it: hours not delivered by the end of the window expire, so an unfilled shift is revenue lost for good, not revenue delayed.

SANDNo authorization record at all.

Payer effective dates, the start and end of the payer’s coverage, hide inside ClientPayerInformation on the client record. To tell whether a visit is billable, you cross-check its service code and date against those client-record dates; there is no authorization record to look up.

HHAXA separate authorizations API.

Authorization data lives in its own feed, beside the visit feed. To check a visit, you pull both feeds and match them up yourself.

NSA reference number on every visit.

AuthorizationID travels on each visit record, but it is only a reference. The feed does not say whether the visit was authorized; you look up the authorization that ID names and check its units and date window yourself.

TXWritten into the visit record itself.

VISIT_AUTHORIZATION_NUMBER is one of the 141 fields. The number travels with the visit.

Why this matters at 3 AMFilling a gap is not only “who is free and nearby.” The replacement visit must fall inside the client’s active authorization: right service code, units remaining, window still open. A replacement who is free and nearby can still produce an unbillable visit if any of those three checks fails, and then the agency pays the caregiver with no reimbursement.

Sandata altEVV v7.10 (ClientPayerInformation); HHAX EDI docs (auth API); Netsmart Implementation Guide; TMHP Appendix C v3.0. Use-it-or-lose-it: CareBravo, “Authorization Management.”

Why authorization is treated as the gate

Two mechanics make authorization a gate rather than paperwork. Payers pay only authorized services: outside the service type, unit budget, or date window, the claim is denied after the agency has already paid the caregiver. And authorized hours expire when the window closes. Both are standard Medicaid home-care mechanics, cited in the source line below.

Given those stakes, where each dialect stores the authorization determines how you have to build validation. If payers in practice routinely paid slightly-out-of-authorization claims, this module would overstate the risk; the sources say they do not.

04Identity: what is “the same visit”?

“Same visit” is a judgment the software has to make, not a fact it can look up. The same hour of care exists at once in the agency’s EMR, the EVV system, and the state’s aggregator, under identifiers that do not align.

FieldHHAXSANDTXNS
Visit identifiers
HHAXexternalVisitID (the agency’s) + evvmsId (the aggregator’s, assigned on arrival)
SANDVisitOtherID (the agency’s)
TXVISIT_VISITID
NSVisitId (the aggregator’s, unique across every agency)
  1. The agency schedules the visit in its EMR, which stores it under an ID of its own.
  2. After the visit, the agency’s EVV system submits the record to the aggregator, with the agency’s ID inside it, in the field the spec reserves for it (externalVisitID, VisitOtherID).
  3. The aggregator stores its own copy and stamps its own ID on it (evvmsId, VisitId). One visit now lives in two databases under two IDs.
  4. If the aggregator’s copy has a problem, a missing clock-out, an unverified time, the visit cannot be billed until a coordinator fixes it. The fix starts with knowing which client, caregiver, and shift the flagged record refers to, and that information lives in the EMR.
  5. When the agency’s ID made the round trip, connecting the two records is a lookup. When it did not, the records can only be matched on their facts: same client, same caregiver, same service day, times within a tolerance (the service day itself depends on a timezone conversion; concept 3 above). Records without the ID occur often enough in production that Zingage’s published matching logic has a branch for exactly that case.

A matcher can fail in two directions. Too strict, and one broken visit shows up as two separate problems, one from each database, so the coordinator gets two tickets for one fix. Too loose, and a broken record gets attached to the wrong healthy visit when a client had more than one that day, so the problem looks handled and nobody fixes it. And unfixed means unpaid: since April 2024, Texas denies any claim for an EVV-required service that does not match an accepted visit, and that match runs on the same kind of facts, Medicaid ID, date of service, procedure code, not on a shared visit ID.

This is a structural property of the industry, not a defect in any one vendor. The EVV mandate put a verification system between the agency’s EMR and the state, so every visit is recorded in more than one system from the start. Each integrator has to decide how much drift to tolerate and which source wins for each field, and the answer differs per integration because rounding behavior differs per system.

Why this matters at 3 AMThe unverified 9:00 PM visit in the aggregator and the 9:00 PM visit in the EMR are usually the same visit under two IDs. Matching code that does not know that pages a coordinator twice for one problem, or never.

Why identity is called a policy decision

Because the identifiers are minted by different systems at different moments: the agency assigns its ID at scheduling, the aggregator assigns its own when the record arrives, and nothing forces them to reference each other. When IDs fail to line up, the only remaining evidence is participants plus time, and time is exactly the part that drifts, gets rounded, and crosses midnight in UTC.

Production code in this industry already works this way. Zingage’s data-platform post publishes its fallback identity check: match patient, caregiver, and service day within an adaptive epsilon (their term for the time tolerance) that “adjusts per integration, based on observed rounding and clock-in drift,” with the service day computed in the business timezone, not UTC. Every clause of that check is a policy choice of the kind this module describes.

Any code that matches records across these systems therefore contains a tolerance decision, whether or not its author made it consciously. The claim here is only that the decision should be explicit.

05Two reason-code families, two different processes

Two of the four dialects cannot say “this visit never happened.” And confusing a fixed visit with a missed one is not a bookkeeping error: one direction bills Medicaid for care nobody delivered, a false claim; the other writes off revenue the agency actually earned.

The empty half of that grid is the finding. In HHAeXchange and Netsmart, a miss is its own record, with reason codes and follow-up actions. In the Sandata base spec the closest thing is a bare cancel flag, and in the Texas layout there is no missed-visit record at all: the state learns of a miss late, through visit maintenance, or never, and the agency detects it from its own schedule (scenario C below plays this out).

The two families run on different deadlines. In Texas, an edit can happen for up to 95 calendar days after the visit, each one carrying a reason code and the name of who made it; Texas calls this correction window visit maintenance. A miss creates duties immediately: cover the visit, notify people, and for Medicare-certified home health, tell the physician when a visit ordered in the plan of care is missed.

Why this matters at 3 AMThe coordinator covering a no-show and the coordinator fixing a dead-phone clock-in are doing different jobs: one has a client at home without care right now, the other has paperwork inside a correction window. A tool that shows both in one merged “exceptions” list buries the urgent job under the clerical one.

Why edits and misses are treated as different objects

Because the dialects and the regulators already treat them separately. HHAeXchange publishes separate code tables for the two (200-222 versus 600-611 in Illinois). Netsmart requires a missed-visit reason only when the status is unbillable. The deadlines differ too: edits are bounded by a visit-maintenance window, while a miss creates duties immediately, including a physician notification for Medicare-certified home health. Merging them into one list erases distinctions the compliance rules themselves draw.

The false-claim framing in the judgment is this guide’s inference, not a quoted rule: it follows from billing being allowed only for verified, delivered visits, and from the anti-fraud purpose of the EVV mandate itself (the CMS deck in the sources). The forfeited-revenue direction follows from the use-it-or-lose-it mechanics in 03.

One caveat: the Sandata and Texas cells in the missed-visit row are absence claims, inferred from what the specs do not say. They are the likeliest cells in this table to be wrong, and the spec wins if it disagrees. The judgment’s “two of the four” leans on exactly those cells.

Texas Appendix Cno record?

Appendix C defines a visit transaction. A visit that produced no calls is not a distinct record type in the layout.

As far as the published layout shows, the miss either enters the state’s view later, through visit maintenance (the Texas correction window described in 05) and reason codes, or never enters it at all.

You detect this case from your own schedule, before this feed is ever involved. The feed will not tell you.

  • HHAeXchange records the miss as its own object with its own code tables, separate from the edit tables. Sandata records an absence plus a cancel flag. Netsmart (not shown) records a status: VisitStatus: UNBL with MissedVisitReason required. Texas may have no record at all. Four different answers for the same non-event (05).
  • Whatever the dialect, the miss is discovered the same way: a scheduled start passed with no matching clock-in. The status and reason fields above are filled in by the agency after the fact; at the moment the visit is being missed, you detect it by comparing the schedule against the clock-ins that actually arrived, never by reading a field (01).

Tasks are the least standardized element. The base Sandata contract, HHAeXchange, and Netsmart all carry a task list, but the Texas layout has none, and Ohio and North Carolina, two states that run Sandata, leave the task fields out of their state variants. A state variant can drop whole fields, not just change code values, so even a ✓ in this row needs a check against the specific state’s spec before you scope a task workflow.

How to read the × and ◑ marks

An × means the published layout has no field for it. That is a claim about what a document leaves out, which is easier to get wrong than a claim about what it says. If you doubt an ×, check the cited spec, and trust the spec over this table wherever they disagree. The two most consequential absences, Sandata’s missing missed-visit codes and the missing missed-visit record in the Texas layout, are discussed where they matter (05 and scenario C above). The ◑ marks are this guide’s call about what counts as partial; the specs do not grade their own fields.

The agency-side blind spot

Everything above is the aggregator’s view. The scheduling EMR where an agency actually lives sees the same visit differently. AxisCare, a widely deployed home-care EMR, publishes a full public API, documented in OpenAPI 3.1, covering clients, caregivers, visits, schedules, activities of daily living, and call logs, and no EVV aggregator file spec at all: EVV capture happens inside its mobile app and flows to the state aggregators on the agency’s behalf.

So the agency’s record of a visit carries schedules, visits, and call logs, but not the proof layout above: no verification enums, no match flags, no reason-code tables. The aggregator’s record is almost nothing but that layout. Reconciling the two views (04) is a routine part of integration work. Zingage’s data-platform post shows the split from the inside: in its field-ownership example, the EMR (AxisCare) owns the schedule and the clock-ins, caregiver metadata belongs to the HR system, and assignment status to Zingage itself. Every field has exactly one writer, and the merged record is put together at read time, each time something asks for it.
